## The fee problem

If each $1 donation is processed as a separate card transaction, Stripe’s standard listed U.S. online card fee is **2.9% + 30¢**, which leaves about **67.1¢** from each $1 before any other costs. ([Stripe][2])

So the real net, if every $1 is its own transaction, looks more like this:

| Base population | 1% monthly net | 5% monthly net | 10% monthly net | 50% monthly net |
| --------------: | -------------: | -------------: | --------------: | --------------: |
|    Phoenix city |     $11,229/mo |     $56,142/mo |     $112,284/mo |     $561,421/mo |
| Maricopa County |     $31,355/mo |    $156,776/mo |     $313,552/mo |       $1.57M/mo |
|   Phoenix metro |     $34,825/mo |    $174,125/mo |     $348,249/mo |       $1.74M/mo |
| Hypothetical 6M |     $40,260/mo |    $201,300/mo |     $402,600/mo |       $2.01M/mo |

That means your instinct is right, but the payment design matters.

A pure **$1 public ask** is powerful.
A pure **$1 card transaction every time** wastes a large percentage.

## Realistic read

The strongest version is:

**Advertise $1. Let people give $1 without shame. Make the platform culturally built around $1. But technically design it so donors can add multiple $1 units in one transaction.**

Example:

| -------: | ------------------: | -----: |
|       $1 |              $0.329 | $0.671 |
|       $5 |              $0.445 | $4.555 |
|      $10 |              $0.590 | $9.410 |

So the public language can stay:

**“One dollar helps.”**

The checkout can allow:

* $1

But the psychology stays dignity-first:

**No shame for $1. No pressure to give more.**

## What this means

This idea is not mathematically stupid.

Even tiny participation is meaningful:

* **0.1% of metro Phoenix** giving $1/month = about **$5,190/month gross**.
* **1% of metro Phoenix** = about **$51,900/month gross**.
* **5% of metro Phoenix** = about **$259,500/month gross**.
* **10% of metro Phoenix** = about **$519,000/month gross**.
* **Half of metro Phoenix** = about **$2.6M/month gross**, or **$31.1M/year gross**.

The real bottleneck is not population.

The bottleneck is:

1. trust;
2. local placement of QR codes;
3. clean vetting without humiliation;
4. payment-fee design;
5. transparent need fulfillment;
6. showing proof that dollars actually become help;
7. preventing the platform from becoming bureaucratic or exploitative.

That is the sharpest version: **trust-first, refusal-without-accusation, low-dollar dignity aid.**

The important design principle is:

**Beacon can deny a request without making a character claim about the person.**

A denial can mean:

* not enough info
* item category not supported
* request amount too high for current tier
* fulfillment path unclear
* duplicate request too soon
* risk needs a second reviewer
* platform does not have funds available

It does **not** mean:

* “you’re lying”
* “you’re on drugs”
* “you’re scamming”
* “you don’t deserve help”

That matters because anxious people, traumatized people, sick people, embarrassed people, and people in crisis can all present “messy.” A system that reads messy as guilty will fail the exact people it claims to serve.

The second-review idea is strong. It takes pressure off the first reviewer and keeps the platform from becoming cold or arbitrary.

Clean model:

**Reviewer 1:** checks request against policy.
**Reviewer 2 / human dignity review:** looks at edge cases before refusal becomes final.
**Final answer:** neutral, no accusation, with a possible smaller/safer alternate fulfillment path.

Example:

**“We can’t fulfill this exact request as submitted, but we may be able to help with a lower-cost version, a restricted store card, or a direct vendor payment.”**

That preserves the person while protecting the pool.

The Amish register example maps to the deeper point: trust systems can work when the culture, stakes, and social norms support them. Beacon would not rely on pure trust everywhere. It would borrow the part that matters:

**start from human trust, then add quiet guardrails only where needed.**

The Facebook group comparison is exactly the market proof. People are already doing this in chaotic form:

* public posts
* screenshots
* Venmo/Cash App
* humiliation risk
* scam risk
* donor uncertainty
* no fulfillment trail
* no privacy
* no repeatable structure

Beacon makes the existing behavior cleaner:

**anonymous request → small need → pooled/community funding → restricted fulfillment → receipt/status → dignity preserved**

The “membership” angle can work if it is not treated like a paywall for desperate people. Better as trust/account structure:

* free recipient account
* donor account
* sponsor account
* local helper/reviewer account
* business/event/Airbnb sponsor account

QR Resets then becomes the low-friction public layer:

**scan → reset page → tiny useful help → donate/sponsor/request → Beacon pool**

That is a coherent ecosystem:

**QR Resets brings people in. Beacon routes help. BNDR builds the system.**
